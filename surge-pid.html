How PID surge pricing works

Marketplace platforms balance supply and demand using surge multipliers — pricing that adjusts in real time to bring more drivers/couriers/sellers online when demand exceeds capacity. The classic way to do this is a threshold rule: if imbalance > X%, set surge to 1.5x. It works, but it overshoots and oscillates because there's no feedback memory.

The smarter approach borrows from control theory invented in 1922 by Nicholas Minorsky: the PID controller. PID stands for Proportional, Integral, Derivative — three terms that together compute the right amount of correction to apply at any moment, given how much error there is now, how much has accumulated, and how fast it's changing.

The formula

error[t] = (demand − supply) / demand × 100 // MAPE as % P_term = Kp × error[t] // react now I_term = Ki × Σ error × dt // remember D_term = Kd × (error[t] − error[t−1]) / dt // anticipate surge[t] = clamp(1.0 + (P + I + D) / 100, 1.0, 3.0)

What each term does

  • P (proportional) reacts to current error. Big mismatch right now? Big surge right now. Used alone, it overshoots and oscillates around the target — like turning a steering wheel too hard.
  • I (integral) accumulates error over time. If imbalance persists for hours, I keeps pressing the surge up. It corrects for steady-state bias the P term can't reach, but too much I creates oscillation.
  • D (derivative) reacts to how fast error is changing. If MAPE is rising rapidly, D anticipates the spike before P fully reacts. It catches sudden surges (airport, weather event) but is sensitive to noise.

Tuning trade-offs

  • The defaults loaded here (Kp=0.4, Ki=0.008, Kd=0.05) are conservative — they outperform the naive 30%-threshold rule on 4 of the 5 scenarios. Storm Event is the hard case where neither approach excels (combined demand spike + supply scarcity).
  • For systems with delayed supply response (drivers take time to come online), smaller gains are stabler. Aggressive Kp causes overshoot. Ki accumulates fast when error persists — keep it small.
  • Increase Kp until you see overshoot in the surge chart, then back off ~20%.
  • Add Kd for sharp spikes (Airport Burst). Avoid Kd if your demand signal is noisy.
  • Field rule: when in doubt, smaller gains. Aggressive PID makes drivers' lives unpredictable, riders' prices erratic, and ops teams confused.

What this simulator is NOT

  • It's not a production-ready surge engine. Real implementations account for many more variables (geographic clusters, driver utilization, ETA targets, regulatory caps, two-sided NPS impact).
  • It's not any specific platform's proprietary algorithm. Production systems at ride-hail and delivery companies are far more complex (geographic clusters, ETA targets, regulatory caps). This is the canonical PID pattern applied to a simplified marketplace model.
  • It's not a substitute for ML-based surge. Modern systems often combine PID-style feedback with predictive ML on top.

What this simulator IS

  • A teaching tool for ops leaders studying the control-theory side of pricing
  • A starting point for implementing PID surge in your own data pipeline
  • A way to build intuition for why naive threshold rules underperform
  • A reference implementation you can read in 200 lines of Python
