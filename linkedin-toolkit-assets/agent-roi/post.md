Budget approved. An ops leader at a global marketplace had sign-off to add two agents to a manual review queue screening 200 flagged orders a day. On paper, the queue made money.

The ROI math everyone trusted: orders times accuracy times savings per catch, divided by daily cost. Gross over gross. The queue read 0.6. Comfortable enough to fund.

Gross hid the false positives. A 6% false-positive rate against $180 in customer lifetime value was burning about $1,400 a day in good customers wrongly blocked. The ROI number never saw it.

She ran it in portfolio mode, on a net basis. Subtract false-positive cost from gross savings. Rank every queue by net savings per agent. Then ask one question: where does the next agent actually pay?

On the net basis, that 0.6 queue read negative. Deeply. She rewrote the decision rules instead of hiring, false positives fell from 6% to 2.5%, net ROI turned positive, and the call saved around $280K against the two-agent hire already signed off.

Adding agents to a bleeding queue does not fix it. It funds the bleed faster. The question was never whether the queue is profitable. It is where the next dollar goes.

Gross ROI says the queue makes money. Net ROI says which mistake you are paying for.
